## Plausible biological underpinnings

1. Vascular injury is central in both sexes  
   Hypertension, hyperlipidemia, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, coronary disease, and kidney injury all point to vascular/metabolic contributions to dementia: small-vessel disease, hypoperfusion, embolic injury, endothelial dysfunction, blood–brain barrier impairment, inflammation, and reduced vascular reserve. The KG linked dementia/vascular dementia to genes such as `APOE`, `TOMM40`, `APP`, `CR1`, and `CD2AP`, consistent with lipid handling, amyloid biology, complement/immune pathways, and neuronal vulnerability.

2. Women: UTI, osteoporosis, thyroid disease, and depression  
   - UTI may reflect older age, frailty, incontinence, estrogen-related urogenital changes, and institutional exposure; infections can precipitate delirium and accelerate apparent cognitive decline through systemic inflammation.
   - Osteoporosis may reflect post-menopausal estrogen loss, frailty, falls, vitamin D/calcium biology, and shared inflammatory/endocrine aging pathways.
   - Hypothyroidism can worsen cognition through slowed metabolism, vascular risk, mood symptoms, and potentially reversible cognitive impairment.
   - Depression may represent both a prodrome and a contributor to dementia via HPA-axis dysregulation, inflammation, sleep disruption, and reduced cognitive reserve.

3. Men: cardiovascular, renal, prostate, and metabolic burden  
   - Atrial fibrillation increases risk of silent infarcts, embolic stroke, cerebral hypoperfusion, and anticoagulation-related complexity.
   - Diabetes and coronary atherosclerosis support a cardiometabolic dementia pathway involving insulin resistance, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation.
   - Acute kidney injury may mark systemic vascular disease, inflammation, toxin/metabolite burden, medication vulnerability, and hospitalization-associated delirium.
   - Benign prostatic hyperplasia is likely partly biological and partly care-pattern mediated: urinary retention, nocturia/sleep fragmentation, anticholinergic exposure, catheterization, and infection risk may worsen cognition or delirium susceptibility.

## Caveats

These are EHR-coded comorbidities, not adjudicated diagnoses. Frequencies reflect documentation, health-care utilization, survival, age structure, and coding practices. The analysis is cross-sectional among patients with dementia, so it does not establish causality or temporal ordering.
