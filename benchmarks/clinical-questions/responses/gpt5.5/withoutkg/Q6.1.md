## Plausible biological and clinical underpinnings

- Vascular-metabolic burden: Hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, CKD, and coronary disease plausibly contribute to vascular brain injury, microinfarcts, white-matter disease, blood-brain-barrier dysfunction, and mixed Alzheimer/vascular dementia phenotypes.
- Sex hormones and aging: Post-menopausal estrogen loss may contribute to vascular risk, bone loss, and possibly altered neuroinflammatory and amyloid/tau biology; this may partially align with the high burden of osteoporosis and cardiometabolic diagnoses in women.
- Infection/delirium vulnerability: UTIs are much more frequent in women and can precipitate delirium, functional decline, hospitalization, and apparent cognitive worsening in dementia; anatomy, post-menopausal urogenital changes, frailty, and care-setting exposure may contribute.
- Thyroid disease: Hypothyroidism is more common in women and can worsen cognition, mood, fatigue, and functional status; it is also a potentially reversible contributor to cognitive symptoms.
- Depression: More frequent coding in women may reflect both true higher prevalence and detection/care-seeking differences. Depression can be prodromal, comorbid, or a risk factor through inflammatory, vascular, HPA-axis, sleep, and behavioral pathways.
- Male cardiovascular/urogenital burden: BPH reflects male biology and aging, while atrial fibrillation and coronary disease may indicate higher overt cardiovascular burden, increasing embolic stroke, hypoperfusion, and vascular cognitive impairment risk.
- Anemia and kidney disease: Common in both sexes; plausible mechanisms include reduced oxygen delivery, inflammation, frailty, nutritional deficiency, and CKD-associated vascular/toxic-metabolic effects.

## Caveats

These are unadjusted EHR frequencies, not causal effects. Results may reflect age structure, encounter frequency, coding practices, survival bias, disease severity, and care-setting mix. Women in this dementia cohort were slightly older on average than men by crude birth-year approximation, which could inflate age-associated comorbidities such as osteoporosis, UTI, and hypothyroidism.
