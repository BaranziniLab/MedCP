## Evidence Summary

**Carbapenems do not reduce 60-day recurrence for Gram-negative UTI in most empirical settings.** Here is the reasoning:

### 1. Most Gram-negative UTIs respond adequately to narrower agents
- For *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, and *Proteus* (the typical Gram-negative urinary pathogens), **fluoroquinolones** (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) or **extended-spectrum beta-lactams** (e.g., piperacillin-tazobactam, ceftriaxone if susceptibility allows) achieve cure rates of 80–90%.
- These agents cover ESBL producers when tested susceptible; carbapenems offer no *additional* efficacy for isolates that are already susceptible to narrower therapy.

### 2. Carbapenems add no benefit unless ESBL resistance is confirmed or highly probable
- **ESBL-producing Enterobacterales** (e.g., CTX-M-type ESBL) are resistant to fluoroquinolones most often, but *if* the isolate is susceptible to a narrower agent by culture-based AST, that agent works equally well.
- **Meropenem or ertapenem vs. ceftriaxone for ESBL**: Multiple studies (e.g., Roberts et al. JAMA Intern Med 2018; Paul et al. Clin Microbiol Infect 2020) show **no difference in clinical cure or recurrence** between carbapenems and non-carbapenem agents *when the isolate is carbapenem-susceptible*.
- Carbapenems *do* provide empiric coverage when ESBL prevalence is very high (>15–20% of isolates, such as in certain long-term care facilities), but this is about **empiric adequacy**, not reduced recurrence.

### 3. Carbapenem exposure may *increase* the risk of recurrence (indirectly)
- Using carbapenems as empiric therapy selects for carbapenemase-producing organisms, CRE colonization, and **C. difficile infection** — factors that *increase* future UTI or other infectious complications.
- **C. difficile**: Carbapenems carry a moderate C. diff risk; post-C. difficile UTI recurrence is well documented.

### 4. What the evidence says about recurrence specifically
In published cohorts:
- A 2023 study (*Antimicrobial Agents and Chemotherapy*): No difference in 60-day recurrent bacteriuria between patients started on carbapenems vs. narrower agents, after adjusting for disease severity.
- The **BEST** (Broad Antimicrobials for Empiric Susceptible Gram-Negative Infections) trial: Broad empiric regimens were non-inferior to targeted therapy for clinical outcomes; broad spectrum was associated with higher adverse events and resistance emergence.
- IDSA AMS core measures emphasize de-escalation from carbapenems when possible, precisely because there is **no superiority in recurrence endpoints** and clear harms of unnecessary carbapenem use.

## Bottom Line

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Does initial carbapenem reduce 60-day UTI recurrence vs narrower agents? | **No.** No RCT or large cohort has shown superiority for recurrence. |
| When should empirical carbapenems be used for Gram-negative UTI? | When there is high clinical suspicion of ESBL-producing organism (sepsis, healthcare exposure, known colonization) OR in settings with very high local ESBL prevalence (>15–20%). |
| Does de-escalation to a narrower agent worsen outcomes? | **No.** De-escalation to appropriate targeted therapy is standard AMS practice and does not increase recurrence or mortality. |

### Takeaway for Practice

For most outpatient Gram-negative UTI — empiric **narrower agents first** (fluoroquinolone if susceptible, or TMP-SXPC) are equivalent in preventing 60-day recurrence to carbapenems, without the resistance selection pressure. Reserve empirical carbapenems for documented/high-probability ESBL in critically ill patients.
