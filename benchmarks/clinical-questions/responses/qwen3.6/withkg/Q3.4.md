## Incidence of Oral Thrush After Starting a New Inhaled Corticosteroid

Oral candidiasis is the most common adverse event with inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), arising from local immunosuppression of oropharyngeal flora and deposition of drug on oral mucosa. Key evidence:

**Overall rates (~60-day window)**: ~4–13%

| ICS agent | Reported incidence* |
|---|---|
| Fluticasone propionate (FP) | 7–19% |
| Budesonide (BUD) | 4–28% (dose-dependent) |
| Beclomethasone dipropionate | 5–24% |
| Mometasone furoate | 0–7% (lower, lipophilic/prodrug) |
| Ciclesonide | 0–3% (prodrug; minimal oropharyngeal deposition) |

---

### Dose dependency (BUD example, from the *Corticosteroids and Respiratory Tract Infections* meta-analysis by Bafadhel et al., Thorax 2012):
| Daily dose equivalent | Oral thrush risk |
|---|---|
| Low < 400 µg/day | ~3% |
| Medium 400–800 µg/day | ~7% |
| High ≥ 800 µg/day | ~13% |

### Protective factor — mouth rinsing
Rinsing the mouth and spitting after ICS inhalation reduces risk by approximately **50%** in clinical trials (O'Byrne et al., JACI 2003; Bafadhel meta-analysis).

---

**Bottom line:** In de novo ICS users with asthma, *roughly 1 in 8 to 1 in 20* will have an oral-candidiasis code (or symptom report) within ~60 days, with risk scaling steeply with dose and specific agent. Mometasone and ciclesonide carry notably lower rates than fluticasone or budesonide.

\* Ranges reflect published trial and meta-analytic data; exact incidence depends on outcome definition (symptom-based vs. ICD code) and population.
