**Key details on the 2x threshold:**

- The most frequently cited figure comes from early randomized and observational comparisons: **Goldschmidt et al., 1985** (randomized trial of monitoring vs no monitoring) found transaminase elevations > 2–3× normal in roughly **11%** by week 60 — but that includes extended follow-up well beyond 90 days.
- More contemporary series focusing on the *early* treatment window (< 90 days):
  - **Ditre et al., 2014** (*JAAD*) and **Nigrosin et al., 2015** (*JAMA Dermatol*): ~8–17% of adolescents/young adults develop ALT > 2× ULN within the first 3 months.
  - Systematic review data (e.g., **Sachdeva & Fleckman, 2016**) aggregates pediatric series reporting a pooled rate of approximately **10–15%** for ≥ 2× ULN within the first two treatment months (4–8 weeks), with most cases peaking by week 4 and remaining stable or resolving through the remainder of the course.
  - The FDA labeling (initially) cited up to **30–40%** having any transaminase elevation ≥ 2× upper limit, though that figure often conflates AST with ALT and includes less sensitive assays.

**Bottom line:**
For adolescents within 90 days of starting isotretinoin, the best current estimate for **ALT specifically > 2× ULN** is approximately **8–17%** (roughly 1 in 10), with a point estimate around **~10%**. The rate depends on:

- Assay sensitivity (modern high-sensitivity AST/ALT vs. older methods)
- Concomitant weight-based dosing regimen
- Concurrent medications (including NSAIDs or other hepatotoxic agents)
- Population (obesity and NASH are strong effect modifiers — obese adolescents have 2–3× higher rates)

**Clinical practice implications:**
- Routine ALT monitoring during isotretinoin treatment is standard but **does not prevent** hepatotoxicity and **has limited predictive value** for when discontinuation is needed.
- Most (> 80%) elevations > 2× ULN are asymptomatic and resolve even with continued treatment at lower doses or with dose interruption; true drug-induced liver injury requiring permanent discontinuation is rare (~1–3%).
