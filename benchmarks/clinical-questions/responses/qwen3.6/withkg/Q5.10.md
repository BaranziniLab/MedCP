## Most Frequent Nutritional Deficiency Diagnoses in the Elderly (>65 years)

### 1. **Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin) Deficiency** — *Rank #1*
- **Prevalence**: 10–30% in elderly, higher with advancing age and PPI use
- **Mechanism**: Age-related atrophic gastritis → ↓ intrinsic factor & gastric acid → impaired release of B12 from food proteins (distinguishing "food-cobalamin malabsorption"). Also common with chronic PPI/H2 blocker use.
- **Key risk factors**: Chronic PPI or metformin use, gastrectomy/bariatric surgery, vegan diet in old age, autoimmunity

### 2. **Vitamin D Deficiency**
- **Prevalence**: ~20–40% depending on latitude, season, and testing criteria (25(OH)D cutoff varies by guideline)
- **Mechanism**: ↓ skin 7-dehydrocholesterol, ↓ sun exposure, reduced dietary intake; many guidelines now use ≥30 ng/mL vs traditional ≥20 ng/mL
- **Clinical significance**: Osteoporosis/fracture risk, sarcopenia associations

### 3. **Iron Deficiency** (with/without anemia)
- **Prevalence**: ~10% in community-dwelling elderly men; higher in institutionalized or frail populations
- **Key concern**: GI malignancy or chronic blood loss — must always be investigated, not assumed "benign"

### 4. **Vitamin B6 (Pyridoxine) Deficiency**
- **Prevalence**: ~20–80% depending on criteria (PLP <10 ng/mL); underrecognized
- **Evidence**: NHANES data shows high prevalence; exacerbated by PPIs, oral contraceptives, isoniazid

### 5. **Zinc Deficiency**
- **Prevalence**: ~10–30% in nursing home and frail populations
- **Evidence**: Decreased dietary intake, malabsorption, medication effects (diuretics)

### Less common but important:
- **Thiamin (B1)** deficiency — alcoholism, bariatric surgery
- **Folate (B9)** deficiency — less than historically due to fortification, but still seen in poverty/alcohol use

### Summary Table

| Deficiency | Approx. Prevalence | Key Elderly-Specific Mechanisms |
|---|---|---|
| B12 (Cobalamin) | 10–30% | Atrophic gastritis, ↓intrinsic factor, PPIs |
| Vitamin D | 20–40% | ↓skin synthesis, ↓sun exposure |
| Iron deficiency | ~10%+ | GI malignancy must be excluded; anemia more common |
| B6 (Pyridoxine) | 20–80% | Diet + medications (PPIs, INH, oral contraceptives) |
| Zinc | 10–30% (NH) | Poor intake, malabsorption, diuretics |

### Clinical Pearls for Geriatric Practice
- **B12 deficiency** is often missed — check if anemia or neuropsychiatric symptoms; remember serum B12 can be falsely normal when MMA/homocysteine are elevated
- **"Food-cobalamin malabsorption"** is specific to aging and is treated differently than pernicious anemia (high-dose oral works because ~1% absorbs via passive diffusion)
- **Iron deficiency in elderly men** should trigger endoscopy/colonoscopy for GI malignancy until proven otherwise
