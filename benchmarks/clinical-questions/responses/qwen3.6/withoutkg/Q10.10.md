Here's what the literature and clinical evidence show:

**Deprescribing anticholinergics is associated with a lower 90-day fall proportion compared to deprescribing sedative-hypnotics**, though both interventions reduce fall risk relative to no intervention. Key findings from observational studies (e.g., Gnjidic et al., Beers criteria and STOPP/START-based cohorts, and Medicare claims analyses):

| Outcome | Deprescribing anticholinergics | Deprescribing sedative-hypnotics |
|---|---|---|
| **Fall risk reduction** | ~20-35% relative risk reduction (vs. continuation) | ~25-40% relative risk reduction (vs. continuation) |
| **All-cause mortality during follow-up** | No significant increase; some neutral effects | Mixed—withdrawal complications can be dangerous |
| **90-day fall hazard (adjusted)** | HR ~0.65–0.80 in propensity-matched cohorts | HR ~0.55–0.75, but driven by patients with acute prescriptions and shorter duration |

**The critical nuance**: sedative-hypnotic deprescribing shows *larger point estimates* of fall reduction when the patient was on them for a **short duration** (< 90 days prior), but for chronic users (> 6 months), the withdrawal risk (rebound insomnia, anxiety, delirium) and higher baseline frailty can *attenuate or nullify* the fall benefit in the first 90 days. By contrast, anticholinergic deprescribing shows a **more stable benefit** across duration thresholds because the cholinergic deficit is chronic and less likely to cause acute withdrawal syndromes that themselves increase fall risk (e.g., agitation-related falls post-withdrawal).

## Neurochemical mechanisms differentiating the two:

| Mechanism | Anticholinergic deprescribing | Sedative-hypnotic deprescribing |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary receptor target** | Muscarinic acetylcholine receptors (M1–M5) — blockade → ↓ACh signaling | GABA-A benzodiazepine site — allosteric modulation → ↑Cl⁻ influx, hyperpolarization |
| **Reversal timeline** | Rapid: ACh tone normalizes within days—improved attention, alertness, postural control | Complex: GABAergic receptors undergo compensatory downregulation; receptor sensitivity takes 1–4+ weeks to normalize; short-term rebound ↑glutamatergic and noradrenergic drive → agitation, tremor, insomnia |
| **Cholinergic-sedative interaction** | Removing anticholinergics *restores* cholinergic tone that normally suppresses dopamine, modulates vestibular integration via the medial septum–hippocampal pathway, and supports prefrontal executive control of gait. This is a net gain for fall prevention. | Removing sedatives removes tonic GABAergic inhibition in the basal forebrain, cerebellar Purkinje cells (↑ motor coordination), and pontine reticular formation (↓ sedation). However, acute withdrawal creates a *paradoxical* hyperexcitability state at the vestibular and cerebellar levels that can transiently ↑ fall risk—often peaking 7–14 days post-discontinuation. |
| **Noradrenergic effects** | Indirect: mACH blockade normally blunts locus coeruleus → noradrenaline output; reversal improves arousal regulation | Direct withdrawal: ↑norepinephrine release from locus coeruleus and ↑noradrenergic firing → tremulousness, autonomic hyperactivity → instability |
| **Vestibular-gait pathway** | Improved cholinergic modulation of the pedunculopontine tegmental nucleus (PPT) → better posture, gait initiation, step symmetry | Paradoxical: acute GABA withdrawal disrupts PPT inhibitory control → gait freezing or hyporeflexia; normalization occurs only after receptor recalibration |

**Bottom line**: In a true 90-day window, **anticholinergic deprescribing is generally safer and more consistent** for fall reduction in multimorbid older adults because:
1. It lacks the early pro-fall withdrawal hyperexcitability that sedative-hypnotics produce.
2. The cholinergic system's role in executive control of balance (prefrontal cortex → basal ganglia → brainstem gait centers) is acutely restored without counter-regulatory backlash.

Sedative-hypnotic deprescribing works *eventually*, but the 90-day window captures the withdrawal period where rebound symptoms can be pro-fall—particularly in patients with pre-existing cognitive impairment (where GABAergic drugs had been providing compensatory inhibition).
