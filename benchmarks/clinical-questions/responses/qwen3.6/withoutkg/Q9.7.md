## 6-month rates of ALT/AST elevations & diarrhea: nintedanib vs pirfenidone

### Diarrhea (most prominent difference)
| Drug | Any-grade diarrhea | Grade ≥3 diarrhea |
|---|---|---|
| **Nintedanib** (INPULSIS-1/2, 150 mg BID) | ~40–41% | ~7–8% |
| **Pirfenidone** (CAPACITY/ASCEND, 2403 mg/day) | ~10–19% | ~2–3% |

Diarrhea with nintedanib peaks in the first weeks of therapy; dose reduction (to 100 mg BID) is required in most patients within the first 6 months for this AE. Pirfenidone discontinuation due to diarrhea is uncommon. Instead, pirfenidone's GI toxicity is driven predominantly by **nausea and vomiting** (~19–43% and ~7–25%, respectively).

### ALT/AST elevations
| Drug | Grade ≥3 AST/ALT (≥3× ULN) | Any-grade |
|---|---|---|
| **Nintedanib** (INPULSIS-1) | 6.8% (vs. 2.4% placebo); INPULSIS-2 similar (~7%) |
| **Pirfenidone** (ASCEND) | 8.9% (Grade ≥3 ALT); CAPACITY: Grade 3+ AST/ALT ~1–2% |

For *any-grade* transaminitis, rates are similar between the two drugs (~20–30%). However, **clinically significant (Grade ≥3) hepatic events** are slightly higher with pirfenidone in ASCEND and lower with nintedanib. Both require regular LFT monitoring per labeling.

## Candidate mechanism for the safety profile difference

The key distinguishing AE — **diarrhea much more frequent with nintedanib** — is mechanistically linked to its multi-kinase inhibition:

1. **Nintedanib inhibits VEGFR2, FGFR1, PDGFRβ** (in addition to RET and EGFR). These receptors are essential for intestinal epithelial homeostasis. Blockade of VEGFR2 signaling disrupts gut microvascular integrity; FGFR/PDGFR inhibition impairs epithelial repair and fluid absorption — together producing high-frequency diarrhea by a mechanism shared with anti-angiogenic cancer therapies.

2. **Pirfenidone has no tyrosine kinase activity.** Its antifibrotic action is proposed through (a) downregulation of TGF-β1 and FGF-2 expression, (b) inhibition of TNF-α–mediated inflammation, and (c) ROS-scavenging properties. These targets do not directly perturb intestinal epithelial barrier or fluid homeostasis — hence the lower diarrhea rate. Pirfenidone's hepatic effects likely stem from CYP1A2 induction and metabolite formation rather than direct organ toxicity.

## Practical clinical implication

Given this safety profile, **nintedanib is preferred over pirfenidone in patients with significant baseline GI disease** (e.g., IBD, prior resections). Conversely, **pirfenidone may be preferred over nintedanib for patients where liver disease or photosensitivity concerns dominate** — though the Grade ≥3 hepatotoxicity risk is broadly comparable between the two. In IPF-ILD with concurrent COPD (a common comorbidity), either drug can be used but diarrhea prophylaxis (loperamide, dose titration) is essential with nintedanib.

Both drugs show ~28–30% FVC decline at 1 year vs placebo; neither has demonstrated a mortality benefit in RCTs. The selection between them hinges on the patient's comorbidity profile and tolerance for the side-effect spectrum above.
