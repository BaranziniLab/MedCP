So in this dataset **rivaroxaban** showed fewer 90-day bleeding admissions.

⚠️ **This is the opposite of the trial/real-world literature** (Caravaggio, SELECT-D, ADAM-VTE, large registries), where apixaban generally bleeds *less* — especially fewer GI bleeds — than rivaroxaban. The reversal here is almost certainly **confounding by indication/channeling**: apixaban is preferentially given to older, frailer, renally-impaired, higher-bleeding-risk oncology patients. It's unadjusted, uses coded admissions (not adjudicated ISTH bleeds), and apixaban's larger n produces more events. Treat it as descriptive, not causal.

## Mechanism (knowledge graph + pharmacology)
- **Same target for the three "-xabans":** apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban all inhibit **Factor Xa (FA10)**; **dabigatran** inhibits **thrombin (FII)** directly. Because the FXa inhibitors hit the same target, between-drug bleeding differences are driven by **PK, not target**.
- **What actually differs:** rivaroxaban is once-daily with **higher peak concentrations** and leaves more **unabsorbed active drug in the gut lumen** → the leading mechanistic explanation for its higher **GI bleeding** vs twice-daily, lower-peak apixaban. Renal clearance also differs (rivaroxaban ~1/3, apixaban ~27%, dabigatran ~80%).
- **Shared liability:** all are **CYP3A4 + P-glycoprotein (ABCB1)** substrates, so bleeding risk rises sharply with strong 3A4/P-gp inhibitors common in oncology (azole antifungals, some TKIs) — a key drug–drug-interaction pathway to check per patient.

To make a defensible causal claim you'd want a **propensity-matched** analysis matching on cancer site (GI/GU vs other), renal function, age, DOAC dose, and concomitant CYP3A4/P-gp inhibitors.
