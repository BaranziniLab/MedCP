## The mechanism half of your question (answerable now)

Independent of the EHR numbers, the neurochemical rationale — and the *prior* most geriatric-pharmacology evidence supports — is that **deprescribing anticholinergics tends to yield the larger fall reduction**, for these reasons:

- **Anticholinergics (muscarinic blockade).** Central M1 antagonism degrades cholinergic neurotransmission that sustains attention, gait control, and postural reflexes; it also causes blurred vision (accommodation) and orthostatic effects. In older brains with already-reduced cholinergic reserve, this produces confusion, psychomotor slowing and unsteadiness — a direct fall pathway. Cumulative anticholinergic burden is dose-dependently linked to falls and cognitive decline. Removing it can improve alertness and gait within weeks.
- **Sedative-hypnotics (GABA-A potentiation; benzodiazepines, Z-drugs).** These raise fall risk through sedation, delayed reaction time, myorelaxation and psychomotor impairment. Deprescribing clearly helps — **but** abrupt withdrawal brings its own transient hazards: rebound insomnia, anxiety, and withdrawal that can *acutely* increase falls in the first weeks, partly blunting the 90-day benefit. Melatonin/orexin agents in that set carry less fall liability to begin with.
- **Net expectation:** anticholinergic deprescribing removes a mechanism (cholinergic disruption + orthostasis + vision) with a cleaner short-term benefit profile, whereas sedative-hypnotic deprescribing is beneficial long-term but can be offset by withdrawal phenomena within a 90-day window.
